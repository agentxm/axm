# How to write a principle document

Start with a decision people have actually struggled with and the people
who make it. Suppose the editors of a community oral-history archive
want guidance for choosing excerpts for their public listening pages.
They already know how to trim an audio file. The hard part is deciding
what a visitor needs to hear to understand a narrator’s account.

## 1. Find two decisions the document should help with

Ask for a recent choice and a case where making the same choice would
cause trouble. Imagine one narrator recalling where her walking club met.
“Our club met at Union Station every Friday,” she says. In a second interview, a
narrator says, “Everyone wanted the station closed,” then adds, “At least
that’s what I assumed before I spoke to the shopkeepers.” Cutting the
second clip at its first pause changes what a listener would attribute to
the speaker.

Ask the editors what both decisions should protect. “Let listeners hear
what the narrator meant” gives them a reason to choose different cuts.
The club member’s sentence conveys a complete recollection. The second
speaker’s qualification tells listeners whose opinion they are hearing.

## 2. Establish what gives the direction weight

Find the applicable decision record, professional guidance, agreement, or
other authority. Check its audience, strength, and exceptions. The Oral
History Association advises users of interviews to put excerpts in context
and cite the full interview. It also recommends honoring restrictions on
access and distribution.[^oral-history] The agreement for a particular
interview establishes which uses its participants authorized.

For this exercise, suppose the archive’s policy permits editors to select
public excerpts wherever the agreement allows that use. Link the actual
policy when drafting for a real archive. Mark a suggested editorial preference
as a proposal until the editors adopt it. If nobody can substantiate an
alleged requirement, leave it out of the obligations the document assigns.
For an inherited maxim, trace its wording to the source and identify whose
interpretation the group adopted.

## 3. Write a passage a reader can use

Name the decision, a reason to choose one way, and a condition that changes
the choice. Distinguish a binding limit from a preference that calls for
judgment. Here is a passage for the archive’s editors.

> **Preserve the narrator’s meaning in an excerpt.** Within the uses the
> interview agreement permits, choose an excerpt that conveys the point
> the narrator makes in the full exchange. Listen on either side of
> the proposed cut. If the speaker corrects an impression or
> attributes a remark to someone else, keep enough of that context for
> the listener to hear whose view it was. Cite the full interview so a
> listener can identify the source.
>
> If the agreement restricts a recording to the reading room, leave the
> audio off the public listening page.

The editors have discretion over the length of an eligible clip. The access
agreement settles a different question. Check that the reader can tell
which choice is theirs and which fact they must establish first.

## 4. Try a case the draft did not grow up around

Give another editor an authorized excerpt that starts with “I certainly
did” halfway through an exchange. Here the missing context precedes the
clip. Ask what they would listen to, where they would begin the excerpt,
and why. If everyone adds the next sentence by habit, revise the passage
until the reader can recognize which words change the meaning.

When working alone, write out competing choices and trace each reason to
the draft or the authority it cites. Record any unresolved policy question
for the person who can settle it.
