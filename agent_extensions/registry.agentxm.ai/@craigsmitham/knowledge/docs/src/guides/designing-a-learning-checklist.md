# How to design a learning checklist

Choose something the learner will eventually have to do independently.
Use the [general checklist guide](designing-a-checklist.md) for how people
will use the aid. Here the writer also decides what thinking to reveal,
when to give feedback, and which support can come away.

## Define the judgment you want to see

Suppose apprentice editors can copy a chart caption but struggle to
write one that the data supports. Give them an invented practice table.
Monday had 12 registrations from 20 invitations, Tuesday had 12 from
30. Decide what you will accept as evidence of the skill. An editor
should identify the comparison, choose the relevant denominator, and
avoid claiming that the day caused the difference.

## Draft prompts that leave work for the learner

Give the apprentice the table first. Ask them to commit to a caption before
revealing the worked answer. On a knowledge-base page, the learner can
expand the answer when ready. On a printed practice card, put it on the back.

> 1. What can you compare fairly across these two days? Write the quantity
>    you will use and explain your choice.
> 2. Check your arithmetic and draft one sentence the figures support.
> 3. Reveal the worked answer. Compare the denominator and the strength of your
>    claim with the worked answer. Revise any difference you cannot defend.

A worked answer could say, *Each day produced 12 registrations. The share
of invitees who registered was 60% on Monday and 40% on Tuesday.* Tell
the coach what feedback to give on a plausible mistake. If the apprentice
writes “Monday’s invitations worked better,” ask which facts would be
needed to distinguish the invitation from differences between the groups.
Keep both the original and revised caption so feedback leads to a visible
change.

## Reduce the support and change the case

When the apprentice explains the first choice, supply a second table with
different totals. Leave only the request for a defensible caption. If the
denominator disappears, restore that prompt on the next practice example
and ask the apprentice to supply the reason. Fading worked support and
varying examples have been studied in other tasks. Use these design choices
as hypotheses to check with your own learners.[^fading][^butler]

Arrange a later exercise with fresh material, the delay chosen for when
the team actually expects to need the skill. For example, at the next
editing session give the apprentice two libraries’ workshop figures. There are
18 of 24 seats filled at one and 24 of 40 at the other. Ask for a caption
and the reason for its comparison without supplying the practice prompts.
A defensible answer can point out that the second library filled more seats
but the first filled a larger share, 75% against 60%. Check the reasoning
and any unwarranted causal claim alongside the figures.

Record assisted performance, performance after feedback, and later unaided
performance separately. If the fresh caption still needs the hidden prompt,
revise the practice opportunities and try a different later case. Reserve
fading for the teaching aid. A checklist that the workplace requires for
real work keeps its operational role.

[^fading]: Renkl and colleagues, [From Example Study to Problem Solving](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510).
