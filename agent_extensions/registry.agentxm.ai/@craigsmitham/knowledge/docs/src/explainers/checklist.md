# What a checklist changes at the point of work

Why give experienced colleagues a checklist for a job they both know?
Consider the handoff between an exhibition registrar and a courier.
At the end of a long day the registrar hands over a sealed crate.
The signed receipt is still on her desk. Both people knew a receipt was
required. Each thought the other had collected it.

A checklist can change this particular moment. Imagine the registrar
opening the dispatch record at the trolley and seeing a prompt.
*Who has the signed receipt?*
The registrar can ask the question while the courier is still there.
The answer can uncover a missing document while it is cheap to fetch.
A tick added later at the registrar’s desk records a very different event.

## What people do with a prompt

A checklist can remind someone to perform a familiar action, or direct
attention to the state left by work already performed. It can also make
two people's knowledge available to each other. The same checklist
might do all three. Degani and Wiener describe flight crews using lists
to configure equipment or to check a configuration afterward. Their
observations also show how the spoken exchange between crew members and
the way a person keeps their place affect the use of the list.[^flight]

In the exhibition example, the registrar knows the crate number. The
courier knows which receipt is in the document wallet. Asking the courier
to read the number back puts the two facts together. How people exchange
the prompt is one choice. What the exchange should establish is another.
If the two people are trying to decide whether the crate may depart,
they also need someone authorized to resolve a mismatch.

## What the marks can tell you

A file full of checked boxes can tell the registrar how many records
were marked complete. To find out whether receipts actually traveled
with the right crates, she needs to look at the handovers or the
documents received at their destinations. To find out whether the new
prompt helped, she needs a basis for comparison.

That distinction has mattered in practice. In the BetterBirth trial the
researchers observed higher adherence to essential practices under a
coaching-based checklist program, while the measured primary health
outcome did not differ significantly between groups. Their result is
about that intervention in its setting. It illustrates why following a
prompt and achieving a downstream outcome are separate questions.[^trial]
