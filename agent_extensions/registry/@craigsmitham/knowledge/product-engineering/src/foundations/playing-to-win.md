# Playing to Win: Lafley and Martin's approach to strategy

*Playing to Win*, developed by A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin, treats strategy as
interrelated choices about the success an organization seeks, where it will
participate, how it will gain advantage, and what makes that advantage possible.
The **strategy choice cascade** expresses those choices. The **strategic choice
structuring process** develops and examines alternatives before selecting one.
The cascade describes the content of strategy; the process helps produce
it.[^cascade][^process]

This explanation is for leaders and product teams who want to understand why
the choices must fit together and how to reason about their credibility. It
uses the authors' public explanations of the approach associated with their
2013 book, with later developments identified where relevant. It is not a
chapter-by-chapter account of the book. Their accounts establish what they
advocate; the fictional rental-service example illustrates the reasoning rather
than demonstrating the method's effectiveness.

For how these choices relate to value creation, landscape, and team problems,
read [Strategy perspectives and their relationships](strategy-perspectives.md).
This explanation develops the choice cascade and the authors' choice-making process.

## Strategy as consequential choice

For Lafley, winning connects superior value for a chosen customer with enough
value for the business to sustain itself. Specifying the customer matters:
choices about customers, location, distribution, offerings, and position in the
value chain determine the business being pursued. Choosing where to stop
participating is part of that reasoning.[^where]

Consider [Northbank Equipment](../northbank-equipment.md), also used in the
[Cagan explainer](cagan-product-strategy.md). “Increase repeat rentals” names an outcome,
but leaves several possible businesses open. The service could become the
dependable choice for contractors whose crews cannot afford a missed delivery.
It could instead become the economical choice for customers able to vary their
dates and collect equipment themselves. Either possibility needs a reason to be
preferred over alternatives and economics that sustain the service.

The choice has consequences. Reserving spare equipment for emergencies may
support dependability while reducing utilization. A service optimized for low
cost might avoid that reserve and pass some savings to flexible customers.
Accepting every request would conceal this tension until staff had to decide
whose promise to break. A strategy makes the intended tradeoff intelligible.

## The five choices form one strategy

The following table paraphrases the cascade. Its order moves from broad intent
toward concrete organizational support.[^cascade]

| Choice | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| Winning aspiration | The success the organization seeks and the motivation for pursuing it |
| Where to play | The customers, needs, offerings, channels, geographies, and value-chain activities in scope |
| How to win | The proposed advantage within that scope |
| Required capabilities | The abilities and activities necessary to deliver the advantage |
| Enabling management systems | The structures, decisions, measures, and practices that build and sustain those abilities |

Lafley's account develops the scope choices; Martin's account explains the
relationships among all five.[^where][^cascade]

The two rental possibilities could be expressed as follows. These are candidate
strategies for examination, not established opportunities:

| Choice | Dependable service for contractors | Economical service for flexible renters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aspiration | Become the preferred local rental partner for contractors who depend on reliable job starts | Make occasional equipment rental an affordable alternative to ownership |
| Where to play | Time-sensitive small contractors, selected equipment, a compact delivery area, direct reservations; operate fleet and delivery | Renters with flexible dates, standardized equipment, online booking and depot pickup; operate fleet and depots without delivery |
| How to win | Earn repeat preference and sufficient price premium through more dependable fulfillment than local alternatives | Sustain lower prices through a lower cost per completed rental than comparable providers |
| Capabilities | Accurate availability, preventive maintenance, reserve capacity, rapid substitution, dependable delivery | Efficient turnaround, demand-based scheduling, self-service pickup, high fleet utilization |
| Systems | Reward promises kept; fund reserve capacity; give staff authority to substitute equipment; review failures across depots | Review cost and utilization; reward efficient turnaround; control service exceptions; price flexible slots consistently |

Martin emphasizes revisiting the choices as a whole. An aspiration may need
revision if no credible combination can support it. A capability gap may force
a narrower scope. Completing five independent lists misses this relationship;
the choices need to reinforce one another.[^integration]

## Where to play and how to win are a paired choice

The attraction of an arena does not establish an advantage within it. Martin
warns against selecting a market first and treating the search for a way to win
as a separate, subsequent task. The proposed scope and advantage must be
developed together.[^integration]

Lafley distinguishes two broad sources of advantage: a sustained cost advantage,
which creates room for lower prices or stronger margins, and differentiation
that customers find meaningfully more valuable. A differentiated offering needs
a match between what it provides and what particular customers value. Merely
being different does not establish that match.[^win]

In the example, “better service” is too vague to explain a win. Dependable
fulfillment matters especially to a contractor paying an idle crew. It may
matter less to someone renting a sander for a project that can move to next
weekend. Restricting the delivery radius might improve substitution speed and
reduce the cost of keeping promises. Here the scope strengthens the advantage.

The economical strategy has a corresponding constraint: a low advertised price
creates no cost advantage by itself. If every renter receives urgent delivery
and bespoke support, the service may simply have lower margins. Its scheduling,
equipment selection, and pickup model must explain the lower cost.

Neither logic removes the other side of the economics. Dependability must earn
enough additional value to pay for its costs; the economical service must remain
useful enough for customers to choose it. These are assumptions of the example,
not conclusions supplied by the labels *differentiation* and *low cost*.

## Capabilities and systems make the strategy credible

Martin includes capabilities and management systems within strategy because
they test whether the proposed advantage is achievable.[^cascade] His
defensibility test asks whether competitors cannot match the offer's value and
economics, or would choose not to because doing so requires giving up something
they value.[^defensibility] Systems must make building and sustaining the
necessary abilities plausible.[^systems]

For the dependable rental service, buying inventory software is a project;
knowing what can be promised, maintaining that accuracy, and recovering quickly
when equipment fails are continuing capabilities. Software may support them,
but depot practices, maintenance, fleet decisions, and staff judgment also
matter. An attractive booking interface cannot compensate for equipment that
does not arrive.

The service would still need to explain why a rival could not easily copy it.
Perhaps it has unusually dense local coverage and a fleet selected for rapid
substitution. Perhaps a broad national competitor would find the reserve
capacity uneconomic under its existing model. Those are possibilities to
investigate, not proof of defensibility. If rivals can match the offer easily,
the proposed advantage is weaker than the original story suggests.

In their 2025 discussion, Martin, Goldbach, and Tuff emphasize that management
systems shape behavior through formal rules and informal expectations. Systems
inherited from an earlier strategy can continue rewarding the old behavior even
after leadership announces a new direction.[^systems]

Suppose depot managers receive bonuses only for utilization. Keeping spare
equipment available now hurts their reported performance, even though it makes
the dependability promise possible. Rewarding staff for every booking accepted
could similarly encourage overcommitment. Reviewing promises kept, sharing the
cost of substitutions across depots, and funding reserves would support a
different pattern of decisions. The management-system choice reaches the daily
conditions under which the strategy succeeds or fails.

## From possibilities to a defensible choice

Martin's process separates imagining possibilities from evaluating their
evidence. Each possibility needs enough of a complete cascade to expose its
logic. Asking **what would have to be true** identifies necessary conditions for
choosing it. Agreement about those conditions can precede agreement that they
actually hold. Conditions that decision-makers doubt become barriers to choice,
directing the testing effort.[^process]

For the dependable service, the conditions and doubts might look like this:

| Necessary condition | Why it matters | Evidence that could change the judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enough contractors value reliability at a viable price | Preference must support the additional service cost | Paid trials or observed purchasing reveal willingness to choose the offer at that price |
| A compact fleet and delivery network can keep the promise | The operating model must deliver the proposed advantage | A bounded operating trial measures failures, recovery times, reserve needs, and full service costs |
| The advantage survives plausible competitor responses | Initial novelty alone cannot sustain the position | Competitive analysis examines local alternatives, response costs, and whether rivals could readily match the promise |

Interview enthusiasm could support further investigation without settling the
first condition. A successful trial in one unusually convenient depot might
leave the second unresolved elsewhere. The economic strategy needs its own
examination: can flexible demand be assembled densely enough to lower costs,
and do the resulting prices attract enough completed rentals?

Martin recommends concentrating tests on the most concerning barriers and
involving skeptics in test design. The purpose is sufficient confidence to make
a choice, with the uncertainty understood; testing cannot guarantee
success.[^process]

### The process has evolved since the book

Martin's 2025 retrospective distinguishes the relatively stable cascade from
successive revisions to the process. The 2013 version framed choices and
generated possibilities. Later revisions sharpened problem definition and
introduced a separate “How might we?” step. In the 2025 iteration he removed
that separate step, made test design explicit, and used *assessment* for
evaluating tests and what would be needed to make an unmet condition true.
These are later refinements, not the original book's exact sequence.[^evolution]

This matters when a capability is missing. Suppose rapid substitution is not
possible today. That may rule out the dependable strategy, or it may identify a
capability the company could feasibly build. A funded, testable path to acquiring
that capability supports a different judgment from an unsupported promise that
operations will somehow catch up.

## Commitment, uncertainty, and learning

Committing means directing resources and decisions behind the chosen
possibility. In the example, that could mean limiting the delivery area,
protecting reserve equipment, and declining incompatible business. It also
means retaining the assumptions that made those sacrifices reasonable.

Consider three later observations:

- Reservations still fail because staff bypass inventory updates. The
  capability or its supporting system needs attention; this does not yet show
  that contractors are unwilling to pay for reliability.
- Reliability improves, but customers will not pay enough to cover its cost.
  The economic premise is now in question, even if the team implemented the
  service competently.
- A competitor introduces equally reliable fulfillment at a sustainably lower
  price. Earlier evidence of customer preference no longer establishes the same
  relative advantage.

These distinctions are an interpretation of the rental example. They explain
why a missed target cannot, by itself, identify which strategic choice needs
revision. Martin's account treats strategy development as recurring work:
choices address current problems, and further problems emerge as circumstances
and outcomes change.[^evolution]

## Capabilities reach beyond application features

For Northbank, the dependable-service choice reaches depot reserves, supplier
relationships, substitution authority, allocation rules, and recovery. It also
requires an engineering system capable of changing those rules without losing
existing commitments. The [allocation change](../engineering/northbank-allocation-change.md)
and [engineering-system change](../engineering/northbank-engineering-system.md)
show those consequences at different resolutions.

The strategy does not select a database or prescribe dependency tiers. Those
choices need local evidence about obligations, integration, cost, and operation.
If that evidence defeats the proposed promise, the paired where-to-play and
how-to-win choices may need reconsideration.
