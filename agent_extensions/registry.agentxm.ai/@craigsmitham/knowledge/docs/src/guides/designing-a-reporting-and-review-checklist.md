# How to design a reporting and review checklist

Bring one report, the criteria its readers rely on, and a decision someone
could reasonably make after reading it. Use the
[general checklist guide](designing-a-checklist.md) for item wording and
the use protocol. Here the design decision is what evidence a reviewer
must be able to find and what happens to a missing or inadequate answer.

## Turn the reporting agreement into questions

Imagine a council reviewing an event closeout report. Its brief asks for
estimated attendance, accessibility issues raised on site, and refunds
for paid tickets. Find out from the council which items apply to every
event and which depend on circumstances. Preserve that authority with the
criteria so a reviewer can distinguish a reporting obligation from a
personal preference. These example criteria come from the imagined brief.

For each reporting item, ask where the reviewer found the answer.[^prisma]
Choose a location the next reviewer can reopen in the format your authors
use, such as a paragraph title, a table row, or a linked attachment.

## Try the questions on the report

Suppose the event was free. Its report mentions a door count and praises
the accessibility volunteers. Try filling in a few rows yourself.

| What the brief asks | What the reviewer can find | Disposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How was attendance estimated? | “Attendance,” paragraph 2, gives the counter total and explains how re-entry was handled. | Coverage located. |
| What accessibility issues were raised and how were they handled? | “Volunteer thanks” names the team. It gives no record of issues, responses, or an explicit account that none were reported. | Open finding. Ask the accessibility lead for the issue record or a supported statement that none were recorded. |
| What refunds were issued for paid tickets? | The ticketing appendix confirms free entry for everyone. | Exclude under the brief’s paid-ticket condition. Record the appendix as the basis. |

Keep the second row open if records are unavailable. A reviewer can record
what the team knows and who can follow up. Treating a silent report as
evidence of zero issues loses the difference between an unknown and an
observed zero. The third row has a different answer because the reader
can trace the reason for exclusion.

For an inspection, test whether the prompts can produce a finding as well
as a location. Suppose the door count includes returning visitors but the
report labels it “unique attendees.” Give the reviewer space to identify
the passage, explain the mismatch, and request either a supported estimate
or a corrected label. Leave room for a consequential concern outside the
listed questions. When assessing whether the aid improved inspection,
compare distinct supported findings, missed defects, and time spent.

## Check the handoff to author and reviewer

Give the report and checklist to a second reviewer. Can they find the
passage, reproduce the exclusion, and tell the author what evidence is
missing? Return the open row to the responsible author. If the report
is revised, reopen the cited passage and check which part of the finding
the change resolves.

Try a nearby case before adopting the checklist. Make some tickets paid
and leave the refund column blank. The earlier exclusion should now fail.
Then make a report that addresses every required topic but gives an
implausible attendance figure. The checklist should let a reviewer flag a
substantive concern alongside complete coverage. Record what the trial
revealed about the aid separately from judgments about this event.
