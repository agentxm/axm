# Practice

A **practice** is a socially sustained, purposive, normatively ordered,
embodied, equipped, and historically transmitted structure that makes a domain
of action intelligible and skillful participation possible. It joins people,
dispositions, activities, artifacts, language, roles, purposes, and standards
of better and worse performance into a coherent undertaking.

Here *practice* does not mean rehearsal, a repeated habit, or a recommended
“best practice.” Nor is it another documentation form. A practice is part of
the lived reality that principles, patterns, rules, guides, and other documents
selectively articulate.

## Orientation

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Central question** | What makes these actions intelligible as participation in one purposive undertaking? |
| **Unit** | A standing social, material, skilled, and normative structure—not one action or procedure |
| **Participants need** | Formation, recognition, equipment, practical judgment, and opportunities to act with others |
| **Documentation can do** | Articulate purposes, language, norms, patterns, procedures, and accumulated reflection |
| **Documentation cannot do** | Exhaust tacit knowing, confer competence or standing, or replace participation and judgment |

## What constitutes a practice

Wrathall's account of Dreyfus identifies a practice with the structure that
sustains action, rather than with the actions considered separately. That
structure has several interdependent dimensions:

- **Purposes and goods** — participants pursue one or more related ends that
  give the undertaking point. These ends may be open-ended: a successful
  performance does not complete the practice once and for all.
- **Normative order** — actions can belong or fail to belong, and performances
  can be better or worse, proper or improper. The practice supplies standards
  of fitting response, not merely statistical regularities.
- **Embodied skills and dispositions** — participants acquire capacities to
  perceive salient features and respond fluently. The practice is carried
  partly in what trained bodies are ready to notice and do.
- **Equipment and setting** — tools, artifacts, spaces, and infrastructure
  embody distinctions and possibilities of action. Their practical meaning
  comes from their place in the undertaking.
- **Social meaning and recognition** — shared vocabulary, roles, statuses,
  examples, and responses coordinate participation. Some actions count only
  when performed by a recognized participant in an appropriate setting.
- **Tradition and continuity** — formation, memory, correction, and renewed
  performance carry the practice through time, including times when no
  characteristic action is presently occurring.

These dimensions should not be treated as an inventory whose parts can simply
be assembled. They are mutually interpreting: purposes make equipment
significant; skills disclose what situations call for; social norms make
actions answerable; and actual performances sustain or revise the shared
understanding.

## Practice, skill, and action

The distinction among practice, skill, and action is foundational:

| Concept | Unit | Relationship to practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Practice** | Standing structure of meaningful participation | Makes a domain of skillful action intelligible and possible |
| **Skill** | Acquired capacity or disposition | Enables a participant to perceive and act fluently within the practice |
| **Action** | Particular deed, omission, decision, or course of conduct | Expresses, develops, violates, or sometimes merely resembles the practice |

An action's physical description does not determine whether it belongs. The
same movement may be incidental outside a practice and meaningful within it;
an improper action may still be answerable to the practice's norms. Conversely,
someone can imitate a movement without occupying the role or pursuing the
purpose that would make it participation.

A practice also persists beyond its current performances. Skilled people,
equipment, institutions, memory, and standing possibilities for renewed action
can sustain it while nobody is presently performing its characteristic acts.

## Background and intelligibility

Practices do more than regulate behavior after a situation is recognized. They
help participants perceive what the situation *is*: what counts as equipment,
evidence, danger, success, a permissible move, or a call for response. Dreyfus
uses **background practice** for practices that normally recede from attention
while making people, things, and situations intelligible.

Not every named practice needs to be classified as a background practice. The
important insight is that focal action relies on a wider background that the
actor usually does not represent as an explicit set of propositions. A
breakdown, conflict, newcomer, or historical comparison may bring parts of that
background into view without making the whole background fully explicit.

## Tacit knowing and formation

Polanyi explains why skilled participation cannot be reduced to rules. In an
integrated performance, a practitioner relies **subsidiarily** on many clues,
movements, maxims, and tool-mediated sensations while attending **focally** to
the meaningful whole. Exclusive attention to the fragments can even disrupt
their coordination.

This is not a contrast between mysterious intuition and communicable
knowledge. Analysis can make some particulars explicit, and explicit teaching
can guide attention and correct error. But the learner must still integrate
what is said in a performance that the words do not determine completely.

Practices therefore reproduce themselves through **formation**, commonly
including:

- explanation and explicit instruction;
- observation and imitation of examples;
- guided participation in real or representative work;
- attempted performance and consequential feedback;
- correction by more capable participants; and
- increasing responsibility for perception and judgment.

Polanyi calls the assimilated use of bodies, language, tools, and conceptual
frameworks **indwelling**. A capable participant ordinarily attends *through*
them to the work rather than treating them as isolated objects. Describing a
practice's equipment should therefore explain what practitioners perceive and
accomplish through it, not merely list what they use.

## Norms, goods, and practical judgment

A practice's accepted norms make performance assessable, but acceptance alone
does not make those norms sound. A practice may organize action coherently
while misunderstanding its goods, excluding people unjustly, rewarding
distorted performance, or damaging the reality to which it responds.

O'Donovan's account of practical reason adds this distinction. Sound action
moves from attentive recognition of goods in the world toward a particular
judgment about what is right to do here and now. Rules can preserve generic
insight and give continuity to practical direction, but they neither generate
the goods they serve nor remove the need for judgment.

This keeps two questions visible:

1. **Internal intelligibility** — does this action belong to the practice, and
   is it competent by the practice's standards?
2. **Responsible truthfulness** — are the practice, its standards, and this
   judgment answerable to the goods and realities they claim to serve?

The second question prevents a practice from becoming normatively
self-authenticating. It also makes criticism and reform internal to responsible
participation rather than treating either conformity or rejection as the only
possibilities.

## Common action, authority, and time

Many practices coordinate differentiated roles rather than aggregating
independent performances. Participants may be authorized to teach, certify,
interpret norms, allocate work, settle disputes, or make decisions that others
can act upon. Such authority enables common action only within a limited field
and remains answerable to the practice's goods.

Decision gives practical reasoning enough closure for action. It may also
establish a new shared context: a precedent, commitment, correction, or
settled direction upon which others can build. In this way each performance
can receive, conserve, develop, damage, or reform an ongoing practice.

Tradition is therefore neither mechanical repetition nor a store of inert
information. It is practical memory handed on through people, artifacts,
language, examples, rules, institutions, and renewed judgments. Fidelity and
change both require practitioners who can recognize what the practice is for.

## Practice and documentation

Documentation participates in a practice when people use it to learn, judge,
coordinate, remember, or act. Different forms make different aspects of the
practice explicit:

| Documentation or guidance | Relationship to a practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Tutorial** | Provides a protected path of guided participation but does not complete formation |
| **How-to guide** | Directs a capable participant toward one concrete outcome |
| **Reference** | Stabilizes explicit facts, vocabulary, interfaces, and distinctions for use in work |
| **Explanation** | Brings purposes, background, relationships, and contested understanding into reflection |
| **Principle** | Articulates a recognized good and durable direction for judgment |
| **Standard** | Establishes a recognized basis for assessing or coordinating a defined field of the practice |
| **Rule or policy** | Makes a determinate demand within a defined authority and scope |
| **Pattern** | Makes a recurring contextual problem and adaptable solution transferable |
| **Playbook or runbook** | Coordinates selection or execution of established responses within an operational practice |

These artifacts can strengthen a practice by making important knowledge
available beyond memory and proximity. They can also expose disagreements
hidden by apparently fluent performance. But no collection of artifacts is
the practice itself. Documentation cannot by itself:

- transmit every tacit discrimination involved in competent performance;
- turn an uninvolved reader into a recognized or responsible participant;
- guarantee that a stated rule will be interpreted fittingly;
- prove that an accepted norm serves a genuine good;
- supply the feedback and consequences through which judgment matures; or
- preserve a practice whose people, purposes, and common action have ceased.

The limitation is constructive. Authors should ask which explicit support will
help practitioners attend and act well, not attempt an exhaustive conversion
of lived competence into text.

## Neighboring concepts

| Concept | Difference from a practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Repeated activity or habit** | Recurrence alone need not establish shared purposes, roles, equipment, or standards of participation |
| **Best practice** | Usually a claimed preferred method; it is better documented as a principle, pattern, rule, or recommendation unless it names the richer structure described here |
| **Standard** | [Recognized basis for judgment or coordination](standard.md); articulates and stabilizes only part of a practice's normative and tacit order |
| **Process or workflow** | Models an ordered flow of work; a practice includes the skills, meanings, norms, and social formation that make the flow intelligible |
| **Institution** | Stabilizes roles, authority, memory, and resources; it may host several practices and is not identical to any one of them |
| **Community** | Names a social body; a community may sustain several practices, while a practice can extend across several communities |
| **Profession or discipline** | Names a broad field that commonly contains interdependent practices, institutions, and bodies of knowledge |
| **Culture** | Names a wider field of shared meaning in which practices overlap and supply background for one another |

## Questions that disclose a practice

When trying to understand a candidate practice, ask:

- What purposes or goods give the undertaking point?
- What actions count as participation, and what merely resembles them?
- What must a participant learn to notice, distinguish, and do?
- What tools, language, spaces, and institutions make the work possible?
- Which roles and forms of recognition coordinate participation?
- By what standards are performances judged better, worse, proper, or
  improper?
- What remains tacit, and how is it learned through example and correction?
- How are disagreement, decision, authority, and responsibility handled?
- What practical memory carries the undertaking through time?
- What can criticize or reform accepted norms when they cease to serve the
  practice's goods?
- Which documents articulate the practice, and which aspects can only be
  learned through participation?

These are lenses for understanding, not required headings or a universal
schema for a “practice document.” Actual descriptions belong with their
subject domains and should follow their host's conventions.

## Failure modes

- **Activity reduction** — defining the practice as whatever actions happen
  repeatedly, without purposes, norms, roles, or meaning.
- **Procedure reduction** — mistaking a documented workflow for the embodied
  and social capacity to perform it fittingly.
- **Codification fantasy** — assuming enough rules and pages can eliminate
  tacit knowing, apprenticeship, or judgment.
- **Equipment census** — listing tools without explaining what practitioners
  attend to and accomplish through them.
- **Norm self-validation** — treating social acceptance as sufficient evidence
  that a practice's standards are good or truthful.
- **Idealized account** — documenting official aspiration while concealing how
  the practice is actually learned, performed, and rewarded.
- **Static tradition** — describing inheritance as repetition without
  responsible development, criticism, or repair.
- **Best-practice equivocation** — using *practice* for ordinary advice and
  importing unwarranted social proof or normative force.
