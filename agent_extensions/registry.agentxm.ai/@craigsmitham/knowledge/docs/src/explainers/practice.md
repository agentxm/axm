# Practice

What does someone need to learn to take part in a **practice**? Think
of playing soccer. Knowing what to do with the ball is part of it.
Knowing whose whistle can stop the game is another. A practice brings
people, purposes, roles, equipment, and standards of good work together.
People acquire its skills, habits of judgment, and shared vocabulary
by taking part with others. What they teach the next participant helps
the practice endure.[^wrathall-background-practices]

Mark Wrathall, introducing Hubert Dreyfus’s account of background practices,
imagines a spectator who runs onto the pitch and kicks the ball into the
net. The spectator might execute a more skillful shot than anyone on the
team. It still counts for nothing on the scoreboard. Only the players in
this match can score. Now suppose a midfielder handles the ball during
play. The referee can call a foul precisely because that player *is*
participating. The skill of kicking, the action on this occasion, and the
practice that gives it meaning are three different things. Calling a
particular coaching method a “best practice” makes a narrower claim.
Coaches might set that method aside and still take part in the same
game.[^wrathall-background-practices]

## What a player learns to see

Imagine explaining a pass to a newcomer watching from the stands. They
see a stretch of empty grass where an experienced midfielder sees a
teammate about to arrive. Freeze the play and a coach can point out the
teammate’s run and the defender who is closing the gap. Once play resumes,
the midfielder has to see those movements together in time to act. The
example gives a sense of what Dreyfus calls *background practices*.
Much of what the players have learned together usually goes unremarked.
It becomes visible when the newcomer asks what the midfielder saw.[^wrathall-background-practices]

Michael Polanyi distinguishes *subsidiary* from *focal* awareness. The
player attends focally to the opening while relying subsidiarily on such
clues as the defender’s change of pace. Concentrating on that defender
alone might cost the player the pass. Polanyi uses *indwelling* for the
way we attend through familiar tools or parts of our own bodies to what
we are doing.[^polanyi-tacit-knowing][^polanyi-indwelling] A coach’s
description can help a player notice a promising run. Playing the pass
too late, seeing the defender intercept it, and trying again teach the
player something that reading the same description again would leave open.

A practice also persists beyond its current performances. The empty
field after the match can host another game because people still know how
to play and officiate, recognize one another in those roles, and maintain
the equipment. Merely preserving the ball and the rulebook would leave
someone the work of learning and teaching the game again.[^wrathall-background-practices]

## What participants can call into question

Shared expectations help a team recognize a good pass. They also make it
possible to question what a club teaches. Suppose a youth coach praises
only the player who scores. A player who passes to a better-placed
teammate helps the team score, yet the habit of praise gives that player
little reason to make the same choice again. Players could learn the
custom perfectly and still have grounds to change it.

Oliver O’Donovan distinguishes recognizing something good from deciding
how to act on it here and now. Rules can carry forward what people have
learned about good action. They also remain open to criticism in light of
the good they were meant to serve.[^odonovan-waking][^odonovan-resolving]
Two judgments follow for the club. The first asks what counts as a good
play within the game. The second asks whether what the club rewards
helps its players learn to play well together. The reasons for praising
the pass can survive a change in the coach’s custom. Praising the pass as
well as the finish changes what the next group of players learns to value.

Documentation participates in a practice when people use it to learn, judge,
coordinate, remember, or act. A [standard](standard.md) can give two
referees a shared basis for making a call. A [tutorial](tutorial.md) can
give a novice a first occasion to recognize one. A written account of a
disputed call can preserve the reasons for a decision after the referee
retires. What each document can carry depends on what its reader can
already recognize, who will give them feedback, and which decisions they
are entitled to make. That dependence matters whenever someone proposes
to preserve a practice by writing it down.
